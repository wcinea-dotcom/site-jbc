 The essentials — what you need to know before reading

The Cayes Botanical Garden has legally occupied and operated a parcel of land leased from the Gérard family since 2002, under a valid lease agreement officially signed before a lawyer. In 2025, Mrs. Marie Donna Bastien Gérard — wife of one heir, and not the sole owner — decided to expel the garden by force, without dialogue, without any serious formal notice, in eight days. She then took control of the site, burned collections of unique endemic plants, destroyed the ecological garden, and planted banana trees in place of a scientific heritage built over twenty-three years. Aggravating the situation: eight legitimate heirs of the Gérard family have officially disavowed her actions and asked her to stop. The Haitian State has not yet intervened. The JBC calls on it to act, in accordance with Article 256 of the Constitution, which makes the creation and maintenance of botanical gardens a constitutional obligation.

Section I — The complete story

Twenty-three years of legitimate work

How a Haitian forestry engineer built, over more than two decades, the only recognized botanical garden in his country — and how it nearly disappeared in a matter of weeks.

A vision born abroad, rooted in Haitian soil

The story of the Cayes Botanical Garden begins with the vision of one man: William Cinéa, a Haitian forestry engineer. After studying forestry in the Dominican Republic between 1995 and 2000, he returned to Haiti with a strong idea — to contribute to reforestation and the protection of nature in a country that deeply needs it. From this, the "Haiti Verte" initiative was born, the precursor to what would become the botanical garden.

In 2002, during a training program in Israel on agricultural and environmental initiatives, William Cinéa visited the famous Bahá'í Gardens — terraced gardens of exceptional beauty, which convinced him that a project of comparable scale could exist in Haiti. Upon his return, he took action: he leased a six-carreau parcel of land located in Bergeaud, along National Road N°2 in Les Cayes, owned by Mrs. Laurette Milard Gérard.

A lease agreement was signed officially and in full respect of legal formalities. Attorney Edlan Jabouin, the family's trusted lawyer, received the payments on behalf of the owners. From the very beginning, the relationship between the Cayes Botanical Garden and the Gérard family was marked by mutual respect and trust. One of the heirs, Jacqueline Gérard, during a visit with her mother, was so moved by the project that she personally proposed replacing the name "Haiti Verte" with "Cayes Botanical Garden" — a clear sign of the entire family's support for this vision.

For Mrs. Laurette Milard Gérard, the lease signatory, the botanical garden represented a natural continuity with family values linked to the land and to reforestation in the South department, particularly in Port-Salut. She regularly visited the garden over the years to encourage the team, offer her advice, and express her support. For more than twenty years, the relationship between the two parties was constructive, respectful, and mutually beneficial.

The JBC's scientific plantations — Botanical collections organized by species, with labeling, documentation and long-term monitoring.
The botanical sections — Each zone of the garden was dedicated to a specific plant family, with scientific protocols for conservation and study.

A relationship becoming complicated — through no fault of the garden

In 2017, the situation changed. Bruno Gérard, one of the heirs who had acquired an adjacent portion of the family estate, expressed his intention to sell the entire property and claim exclusive ownership. A formal email promise of sale was sent by his lawyer. Facing this development, the Cayes Botanical Garden team, concerned about protecting its long-term mission, asked the Haitian State to acquire the land — a step fully consistent with Article 256 of the Constitution, which obliges the State to create and maintain botanical gardens.

Official negotiations began between the State, represented by ANAP (National Agency for Protected Areas), and Bruno Gérard, accompanied by his wife Marie Donna Bastien. An agreement in principle was reached: the State committed to including the acquisition of the land in its national budget and moving forward on the matter. This meeting, documented in an official report and an audio recording, constitutes direct evidence of the State's commitment.

But things stalled. From 2018 to 2025, Bruno Gérard never provided the supporting documents needed to finalize the sale. The reason, according to several family members: the land occupied by the garden does not fall under Bruno Gérard's exclusive ownership, but rather is an undivided estate shared among nine heirs. None of them, individually, can decide to sell alone — let alone to expel. During those years, the Cayes Botanical Garden continued its activities, honored its contractual obligations, and maintained its working relationship with the family. No one challenged the lease. No one asked the garden to leave.

2025 — Expulsion in eight days. Without dialogue. Without legal grounds.

In 2024, Mrs. Laurette Milard Gérard — the lease signatory, the woman who had supported the garden from day one — passed away. Her death changed the balance. And it was in this context that Mrs. Marie Donna Bastien, wife of Bruno Gérard, decided to act alone.

In July 2025, without any prior letter, without a meeting, without serious formal notice, Mrs. Donna Bastien mandated the Thimothé law firm to demand the expulsion of the Cayes Botanical Garden. The deadline she granted: eight days. Eight days to leave a place where twenty-three years of scientific work had been built. Eight days to empty a national heritage recognized worldwide. On July 23, 2025, the police, a judge, and other officials arrived on site and physically closed the garden. No dialogue had taken place. No amicable agreement had been attempted.

After Mrs. Donna Bastien's takeover — Aerial view showing the extent of cleared and burned areas. The photos were taken by drone and officially recorded by a Justice of the Peace.

On September 3, 2025, Mrs. Donna Bastien took a further step. She took total control of the site with her own personnel, erected new fences and barriers, and began what can only be described as the systematic destruction of a national heritage. The rare, endemic, native plants that the JBC's scientific team had patiently collected, studied, and protected for more than two decades were cut and burned — treated like common weeds. The ecological garden, the sacred space of the site, was completely cleared and set on fire. Collections of irreplaceable medicinal and aromatic plants went up in smoke. And in place of these unique botanical collections in Haiti, Mrs. Bastien installed banana plantations.

Active flames — Documented fire outbreaks on the ecological garden site.
Charcoal made from the garden plants — Sacks of charcoal produced with medicinal and ethnobotanical plants.

Faced with this situation, the eight legitimate heirs of the Gérard family — those who never mandated Mrs. Bastien — reacted officially. Through their law firm, they sent her a formal demand to cease her actions. They also wrote to the Minister of Justice requesting his direct intervention. But Mrs. Donna Bastien did not stop.

She added another strategy to her destructive actions: she sent successive summons to the Botanical Garden in an attempt to force it to appear in court. Twice, neither she nor her lawyers appeared at the hearings. On April 7, 2026, the garden's lawyers argued the case. The judge referred the case to another jurisdiction. After the session, the JBC's lawyers made a difficult but necessary decision: they changed all the locks at the site to put an end to Mrs. Bastien's destructions. The Cayes Botanical Garden is now waiting for the Haitian State to intervene, meet with the Gérard family as a whole, and take the decisions needed to save what remains.

Section II — A national necessity

Why this garden concerns the entire Haitian nation

To understand what Haiti is about to lose, we must first understand what a botanical garden represents — in the world, in the Caribbean, and above all in Haiti.

Botanical gardens: a fundamental institution for centuries

Botanical gardens are not simply green spaces where one comes to stroll on a Sunday. For several centuries, they have been institutions of scientific research, biodiversity conservation, public education, and the transmission of knowledge. The first modern university botanical garden recognized in the world was created in Padua, Italy, in 1545, for the scientific study of medicinal plants. That garden still exists today, nearly five centuries later — which says everything about the lasting and permanent value of this type of institution.

Today, globally, botanical gardens play an increasingly crucial role in facing three simultaneous crises: the climate crisis, which disrupts ecosystems and threatens thousands of species; the biodiversity crisis, which is seeing plant species disappear at an alarming rate; and the knowledge crisis, which is witnessing the irreversible loss of traditional knowledge about plants. According to Botanic Gardens Conservation International (BGCI), there are currently 1,775 botanical gardens and arboreta in 148 countries, with many more in planning. These institutions are irreplaceable actors in the protection of life on our planet.

In the Caribbean, Haiti is dangerously behind

Looking at the Caribbean region and its neighbors, the contrast is striking. Cuba has a National Botanical Garden covering approximately 600 hectares, part of a national network of gardens with a scientific and educational mission. The Dominican Republic, the island we share, has a national botanical garden of between 120 and 200 hectares — an institution of national scope, recognized and funded by the State.

And Haiti? The Cayes Botanical Garden, with its six carreaux (about 7.7 hectares), is the only botanical garden in the entire country recognized by the international BGCI network. Just one. In a country of more than 11 million inhabitants, with exceptional biodiversity and one of the highest deforestation rates in the world. And this unique garden is today closed, being destroyed, and no one in the state apparatus has yet intervened to stop it.

In a country marked by accelerated environmental degradation, loss of biodiversity, soil erosion, the disappearance of traditional knowledge, and a chronic lack of appropriate scientific infrastructure, a botanical garden is not a luxury. It is an absolute national necessity.

 Article 256 of the Haitian Constitution — an obligation, not an option

"Within the framework of environmental protection and public education, the State has the obligation to create and maintain botanical and zoological gardens on the national territory."

Saving the Cayes Botanical Garden is not an act of generosity on the part of the Haitian State. It is the application of a constitutional obligation. Article 256 of the Haitian Constitution is clear, unambiguous and without exception: the State has the obligation to create and maintain botanical gardens. Allowing the destruction of the country's only botanical garden without intervening is a direct violation of this constitutional duty.

But beyond the Constitution, this is also a question of the future. Saving the JBC means laying the foundations of a national model. It means demonstrating that Haiti is capable of protecting its scientific institutions, valuing its unique biodiversity, and building a sustainable relationship with its natural environment. It means inspiring the creation of other botanical gardens in the various departments of the country. It means offering future generations a living, useful institution, rooted in Haitian reality.

Section III — The Cayes Botanical Garden

Mission, vocation and the five spaces of the garden

To understand what has been destroyed, we must first understand what the Cayes Botanical Garden was — and what it still is, for what can be saved.

Mission and vocation

Established in 2002, the Cayes Botanical Garden is an institution whose mission is to contribute to human health and the protection of nature. It was designed to be the country's brain for data collection on native, endemic, medicinal, aromatic and threatened plants.

Through its structure, its longevity and its mission, the Cayes Botanical Garden represents far more than a simple garden. It constitutes a major scientific, ecological, educational and cultural heritage for Haiti, recognized around the world.

The five spaces of the garden

The garden is physically organized into five main spaces, all essential to its mission.

1. Ecological space

The health of nature is fundamental to the well-being of humans and plants. This ecological space is a pillar of our mission: to help the Haitian population understand ecosystems, ecology and biodiversity for our well-being. It is designed to reconstitute and demonstrate the functioning of natural ecology.

According to international principles, a botanical garden must devote 30 to 60% of its area to a natural zone with an educational purpose. For more than 23 years, the JBC has worked to build such a space — a bird sanctuary covering 16% of the site, where noise and cutting are forbidden, and where the removal of exotic plants is constant.

The JBC's ecological space — Zone for reconstituting local ecosystems, bird and wildlife sanctuary, a sacred space of silence and contemplation.

2. Conservation and adaptation space (Conservatory)

This space is dedicated to the planting of endemic and native plants of the country, with data collection on reproduction, growth and interaction with wildlife. A section is dedicated to the collection of native and traditional fruit trees of the country.

The Botanical Conservatory — Collection of native and endemic plants of Haiti, patiently built up over the years by the JBC's scientific team.

3. Ornamental and event space

This space is designed to host educational and cultural events, and to collect plants important for landscaping and the development of horticulture. It is laid out to inspire visitors and the country's decision-makers.

The ornamental space — A space of inspiration and beauty, also used for educational, cultural and community events.

4. Ethnobotanical garden

This garden is directly part of our mission to contribute to human health. According to the Ministry of Public Health, approximately 80% of Haitians use medicinal plants for treatment. However, there is very little information on the benefits of species, on which ones are toxic, or on their location and distribution.

Through this garden, our work consists of collecting, studying and documenting the ethnobotanical uses and phytochemical molecules of medicinal and aromatic plants, in order to promote the transfer of knowledge between traditional knowledge and scientific approaches. It also included a palm collection set up to better showcase their diversity and their ecological, cultural and economic importance.

The ethnobotanical garden — Collection of medicinal, aromatic and ritual plants of the Haitian heritage.
The ethnobotanical garden — aerial view (before destruction) — Scientific layout dedicated to human health and the transmission of traditional knowledge.

5. Nursery

The entire scientific value of the JBC is summed up in research on plants: developing protocols to know how to reproduce them, to safeguard them for in-situ and ex-situ conservation, and for ecological restoration. As the operational heart of the JBC, the nursery is dedicated to the production of plants for conservation, ecological restoration, education and outreach. It constitutes an essential refuge for rare, endemic, useful or threatened species, which require monitoring, care and protection.

The JBC nursery — The operational heart of the institution, dedicated to the multiplication of rare and endemic plants for conservation and ecological restoration.

1. Ecological garden — clearing and fire

The JBC's ecological garden was a unique demonstration space, dedicated to reconstituting local ecosystems and raising public awareness of Haitian biodiversity. If you have visited the garden, you know that speaking was forbidden there: it was a sacred space, dedicated to silence, contemplation and the protection of wildlife.

The first actions attributed to Mrs. Marie Donna Bastien Gérard consisted of cutting down all the plants of this heritage and completely burning them. More than a hectare of land was eliminated, more than 200 species of native and endemic plants went up in flames, and hundreds of sacks of charcoal were produced from them. The fire outbreaks documented below show the scale and systematic nature of the destruction: this is not a one-off clearing, but an extensive, methodical and irreversible operation.

The garden today risks losing its status as a botanical institution. Without a natural space representing 30 to 60% of its surface — the internationally recognized standard for botanical gardens — it will no longer be able to claim an ecological vocation. It will take more than 30 years to rebuild this space.

2. Ethnobotanical garden — vandalism and charcoal production

The ethnobotanical garden, dedicated to the preservation of medicinal, aromatic and ritual plants of the Haitian cultural heritage, has been vandalized. Palms and other collection plants have been cut down or damaged. Leaves are used for charcoal production, and some medicinal plants are cut like weeds — uses strictly contrary to the scientific and educational vocation of the site.

The absence of care, cleanliness and monitoring for several months has accelerated the progressive disappearance of this plant and cultural heritage. This destruction represents an irreparable loss for the transmission of Haitian ethnobotanical knowledge.

Medicinal plants and palms turned into charcoal — Ethnobotanical species used for charcoal production, contrary to their scientific and cultural vocation.

3. Botanical conservatory — systematic elimination of endemics

The JBC's botanical conservatory hosted more than 500 native and endemic plants of Haiti, including several rare, threatened or strictly endemic island species. These collections, patiently built by the scientific team over the years, represented a leading national botanical capital and contributed to Haiti's commitments on biodiversity conservation.

Their systematic elimination constitutes a major scientific loss, not compensable in the short or medium term, and deprives the country of an essential tool for preserving its unique natural heritage. Some of these species, having disappeared from the conservatory, are no longer present anywhere else under conditions allowing their study and multiplication.

4. Ornamental garden — abandonment and degradation

This space, designed to inspire and educate, also hosted educational, cultural and community events (weddings, meetings, educational activities). It is today heavily degraded. Lacking care, monitoring and maintenance, several plants are losing their form, aesthetic value and educational function. A living place of connection between nature and society is now disappearing under the effect of abandonment.

5. Banana plantation — misappropriation of a public site

Structuring spaces of the garden have been diverted from their scientific vocation and converted into a banana plantation, transforming a public conservation site into an ordinary agricultural operation. This substitution — from a research and conservation space into a subsistence crop — distorts the public and scientific vocation of the site and destroys years of work. It also constitutes a de facto misappropriation of the heritage purpose of the land.

Banana plantation installed in place of the JBC's botanical collections — Misappropriation of a public conservation site into private agricultural operation.

6. Nursery — dilapidation and agony of rare species

The nursery — the operational heart of the JBC for the multiplication of plants, reforestation programs and the production of plants destined for partners and the community — has been left in an advanced state of dilapidation: damaged structures, lost plants, production infrastructure out of service.

Among the most emblematic species that were conserved there is Clavija domingensis, a plant of very great scientific and conservation value. Lacking care, monitoring and maintenance, Clavija domingensis is dying. Through this species, the full gravity of the situation appears: when the nursery is abandoned, it is not only plants that are lost, but also rare species, years of work and a part of the national botanical heritage.

The JBC nursery — plants of rare species left without care or maintenance — Years of scientific work lost due to lack of monitoring and staff access to the site.
